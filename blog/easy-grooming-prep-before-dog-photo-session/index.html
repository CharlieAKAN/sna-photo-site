Easy Grooming Prep Before a Dog Photo Session

A calm, simple grooming plan can help your dog feel comfortable and look like themselves before a Marietta pet photography session.

September 14, 2026
Snout notes

Relaxed sessions begin before photo day. A little preparation helps your pet arrive ready to explore.

Plan your session

Most dogs do not need a perfect spa day before portraits. They just need to feel clean, comfortable, and like themselves.

That is good news if your dog is wiggly, brush-shy, or not thrilled about nail trims. A photo session usually goes better when grooming is gentle and familiar, not rushed at the last minute. The goal is not to change how your dog looks. The goal is to help them arrive relaxed, with a tidy coat, clear eyes, and nothing itchy or distracting.

We see this a lot in AKC guidance on photographing dogs. Dogs tend to photograph best when they are comfortable in a place that feels safe, and when the session stays fun with rewards and familiar routines. That same idea applies to prep at home.

Start a few days ahead, not an hour before

If your dog already enjoys brushing and handling, great. If not, give yourself a few short practice sessions over several days. The American Kennel Club recommends getting dogs used to calm handling a little at a time, with praise and treats, instead of trying to do everything in one long session.

That means you can break grooming into small pieces:

  • Brush the shoulders and back today
  • Wipe around the eyes tomorrow
  • Check paws and nails the next day
  • Practice standing still for a few seconds at a time

Short sessions are often easier on the dog and easier on you. They also help avoid the tense feeling that can carry into portraits.

Use reward-based handling

If your dog gets uneasy when you reach for a brush, towel, or comb, slow down. Reward-based training is the safest general approach. The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior states that reward-based methods should be used for dog training and behavior work, and that aversive methods should not be used. That matters for grooming prep too, because forcing the issue can create more stress around touch and tools.

A simple pattern works well:

  • Show the brush
  • Give a treat
  • Touch briefly
  • Give another treat
  • Stop before your dog feels done

AAHA also suggests practicing calm handling of paws, ears, mouth, and body for a few seconds at a time while rewarding with treats or brief play. For some dogs, a lick mat or stuffed food toy can help them stay settled during gentle brushing or wiping.

What to clean up before photos

You do not need a complicated checklist. A few small things make a real difference in portraits:

Brush out loose hair

A light brush helps smooth the coat and cuts down on stray fuzz, especially on dark fabric backdrops or around the edges of a sunlit outdoor portrait.

Wipe the face

Use a soft, pet-safe wipe or damp cloth to clean sleep from the eyes and food from the muzzle. Be gentle. If your dog is sensitive around the face, do a quick wipe, reward, and stop.

Check the beard and chest

Dogs with longer facial hair or feathering can hold water, drool, and little bits of leaves. A quick comb-through keeps those details from stealing attention in close portraits.

Tidy the paws

Clean paws matter more than many people expect. Georgia red clay has a way of showing up in photos. If your dog is comfortable with handling, wipe paw pads and trim excess fur around the feet if that is part of your normal routine.

Clip nails only if your dog is already comfortable

If nail trims are easy for your dog, fine. If nail trims are stressful, skip doing a full trim right before the session. It is better to have slightly longer nails than a dog who arrives overwhelmed.

Do not save every hard task for the same day

This is the biggest mistake we see. A bath, blow dry, nail trim, ear cleaning, car ride, and brand-new bandana all in one afternoon can be too much for some dogs.

If your dog needs a full groom, try to schedule it early enough that they can settle back into their usual look. For some coats that may be the day before. For others it may be several days before. If your dog gets the zoomies or the sulks after grooming, give them time.

And if your dog hates brushing, AKC guidance on husbandry training is helpful here. Their advice is to keep sessions short, pair handling with praise and treats, and let the dog step away instead of chasing them down. That kind of choice can make grooming less of a battle and more of a conversation.

Outdoor sessions need one extra check

If your session is outdoors, do one last once-over before you leave home. Look for wet whiskers, leaf bits in the tail, and dusty paws.

If you plan to stop at a park first, keep leash rules in mind. For example, the City of Marietta lists parks and facilities on its official site, and Elizabeth Porter Park posts that pets must remain on a leash and owners should clean up after them. Those simple rules help keep locations pleasant for everyone and make photo time smoother too.

For official park information, you can review the City of Marietta parks page at Marietta Parks & Recreation Facilities and the posted park rules for Elizabeth Porter Park.

Skip anything that could make your dog uncomfortable

A photo session is not the time to test a new shampoo, a tight outfit, or a strong-smelling spray. If your dog normally wears a collar, bring the collar they know. If they never wear bows, they do not need bows for portraits.

The best expressions come from comfort. AKC notes that dogs show their best self when the experience is fun and pressure stays low. We agree with that completely.

When to ask for outside help

If your dog is very sensitive about touch, panics during grooming, or has painful skin, ear, or nail issues, talk with your veterinarian or a qualified reward-based trainer before pushing through prep at home. General session advice can help, but it does not replace medical or behavior care.

A simple pre-session plan

  • Two to five days before, do short brushing and handling practice
  • The day before, clean up the coat, face, and paws
  • On session day, do only light touch-ups
  • Bring favorite treats and keep the mood easy
  • Let your dog arrive feeling rested, not over-managed

That approach keeps the focus where it belongs: on your dog’s expression, personality, and the bond you share.
