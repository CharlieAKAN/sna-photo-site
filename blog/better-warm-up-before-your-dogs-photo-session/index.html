A Better Warm-Up Before Your Dog’s Photo Session

A simple pre-session routine can help your dog arrive calmer, more focused, and ready for relaxed portraits without overdoing the day.

November 9, 2026
Snout notes

Relaxed sessions begin before photo day. A little preparation helps your pet arrive ready to explore.

Plan your session

A better way to use the hour before photo day

There is a big difference between a dog who arrives wired and a dog who arrives ready. We see it all the time in Marietta pet photography. Many people try to help by squeezing in a long walk, a hard game of fetch, a bath, and a rushed car ride right before the session. The intention is good. The result is not always what you hoped for.

A better plan is a warm-up, not a workout. The goal is not to make your dog tired at all costs. The goal is to help your dog feel safe, comfortable, and able to focus for short stretches.

The American Kennel Club notes that dogs photograph best when the session feels fun and familiar, and when you bring favorite toys, rewards, and simple games that fit your dog’s personality. That matches what we see in real sessions too. See the AKC guide here: https://www.akc.org/expert-advice/lifestyle/taking-photos-of-your-dog/.

Start with the routine your dog already knows

Dogs usually do better when the day feels normal. The ASPCA advises keeping training steps clear and building success in small pieces instead of adding a lot of excitement all at once. Their guidance on teaching a dog to go to a spot and stay shows how much dogs benefit from repetition, reward, and manageable distractions. You can read that here: https://www.aspca.org/pet-care/dog-care/common-dog-behavior-issues/barking.

On photo day, that means your usual breakfast, your usual potty break, and your usual walk rhythm often work better than a big special outing. If your dog skips breakfast when excited, do not force it. If your dog loves breakfast, keep it normal and save part of the meal or a few high-value treats for the session if that works for their stomach.

Try to keep the morning quiet. If your dog gets overstimulated by visitors, errands, or busy sidewalks, protect their energy a bit. Calm is more useful than hype.

Use a short sniff walk, not a cardio event

If your dog enjoys walks, a loose, sniffy walk is often the best warm-up. Think 15 to 25 minutes for many dogs, adjusted for age, health, breed, and weather. Let them sniff. Let them settle. Let them move their body without turning the outing into a fitness test.

Why does this help? A gentle walk takes the edge off, gives your dog a bathroom break, and helps them arrive with their brain switched on. A hard workout right before portraits can backfire. Some dogs come in panting heavily, too distracted to think, or too physically tired to hold simple positions comfortably.

This matters even more for puppies, flat-faced breeds, and older dogs. Keep the warm-up easy and kind.

Practice three useful skills for one minute each

You do not need a long training session right before photos. In fact, short is better. Pick two or three easy behaviors your dog already knows and reward them generously. Good options are:

  • name response
  • sit
  • down
  • hand target
  • chin up or look at you
  • settle on a mat or small blanket

The ASPCA’s step-by-step spot training is a good reminder that staying in one place is built gradually and should be rewarded often. A tiny refresher at home can help your dog remember that listening pays well.

Stop while your dog is still happy. We want confidence, not drilling.

Pack comfort, not clutter

The AKC recommends bringing treasured toys and rewards to help a dog relax and show personality in photos. We agree. A small bag is enough:

  • soft treats your dog loves
  • one quiet favorite toy
  • water and a bowl
  • waste bags
  • a towel or blanket with home scent
  • any needed medication your veterinarian has already prescribed for routine use

The American Veterinary Medical Association also advises helping pets get used to crates, carriers, and travel restraints ahead of time, and says a familiar toy or blanket can help pets feel more comfortable during travel. Their pet travel guidance is here: https://www.avma.org/public/PetCare/Pages/Traveling-with-Your-Pet-FAQs.aspx.

If your dog rides in a crate or with a restraint, do a few easy practice rides before the session day if car travel is not already routine. The AVMA also recommends never leaving pets unattended in a car and securing dogs safely during travel.

Make the car ride part of the calm plan

Some dogs do beautifully during the session itself but lose their footing during the drive over. If that sounds familiar, the ride matters just as much as the portraits.

The AVMA recommends giving pets time to get used to their carrier or restraint and to practice car travel before a bigger outing. A familiar blanket can help. For cats, the AVMA specifically says to use a carrier during car travel, but for dogs the same idea applies in spirit: safer travel usually creates a more predictable start.

If your dog drools, trembles, vomits, or panics in the car, do not guess your way through it. Talk with your veterinarian before photo day. General tips online are not a replacement for advice about your individual dog.

Skip last-minute changes if you can

Photo day is not the best time to test a brand-new harness, switch foods, try a strong grooming product, or ask for a long list of behaviors your dog does not know yet. Familiar gear is usually best. Familiar rewards are usually best. Familiar expectations are definitely best.

If you want a polished collar or bandana for portraits, let your dog wear it around the house in advance so it does not feel strange on session day.

If your dog is worried in new places

Fear Free Happy Homes suggests letting dogs safely explore a studio space, camera, and lighting setup when possible, and even testing lighting if a dog may be sensitive to it. They also note that if there is not enough time for that process, an outdoor session may be the better fit. Their article is here: https://www.fearfreehappyhomes.com/professional-photoshoot/.

That is a helpful framework. If your dog is cautious, we can slow the first few minutes down. We can let them sniff. We can keep your asks simple. Sometimes the best early images happen after your dog realizes nothing bad is being asked of them.

And sometimes the best choice is a different setup entirely. A thoughtful pet photo session should fit the dog in front of the camera, not force the dog to fit the plan.

Our favorite simple photo-day formula

For many dogs, this works well:

  • normal meal schedule
  • gentle potty and sniff walk
  • one minute of easy cues with rewards
  • quiet loading into the car
  • safe restraint for travel
  • arrive a few minutes early, not rushed
  • bring treats, water, and one comfort item

That routine is simple on purpose. It leaves room for your dog to think, settle, and be themselves. And that is where the good expressions live.
