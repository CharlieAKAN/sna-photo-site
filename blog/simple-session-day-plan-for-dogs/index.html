A Simple Session-Day Plan for Dogs

A calm, practical plan for the day of your dog’s photo session, from car safety to treats, pacing, and small comforts that help.

November 16, 2026
Snout notes

Relaxed sessions begin before photo day. A little preparation helps your pet arrive ready to explore.

Plan your session

A better plan for the day of your dog’s photo session

Getting ready for pictures is not only about grooming or practicing cues. The day itself matters too. A rushed car ride, a skipped water break, or treats that are too big can change how your dog feels once the camera comes out.

We wanted to write something more specific than the usual prep list. This is a simple session-day plan you can use at home, in the car, and right before we start. It is built around safety, comfort, and realistic expectations, not perfect behavior.

If your dog already knows a few easy cues, great. If not, that is fine too. Calm and comfortable usually matter more than flashy tricks during a Marietta pet photography session.

Start with an unhurried morning

Try to keep the day as normal as you can. Dogs often do better when meals, potty breaks, and rest happen close to their usual routine. Sudden changes can make some dogs feel keyed up or unsettled.

If your dog enjoys a walk, a short sniffy walk can help take the edge off without tiring them out. We do not mean a hard workout. In warm weather, the ASPCA advises pet owners not to over-exercise and to be careful in the heat, especially because dogs can heat up quickly and paw pads can burn on hot ground. You can read their warm-weather guidance here: ASPCA hot weather safety tips.

For many dogs, the sweet spot is gentle movement, time to sniff, a potty break, and then a chance to rest before leaving.

Use food rewards, but keep them small

Treats help a lot during dog photography in Marietta, especially for eye contact, staying close, and building a happy rhythm. But session-day treats work best when they are tiny and easy to chew.

Fear Free Happy Homes recommends treats that are no larger than half a pea or a single lick when preparing pets for potentially stressful visits. That advice translates well to photo day because it keeps the reward quick and helps avoid filling your dog up too fast. Their prep guide also suggests familiar bedding and other comfort items, which can help some pets settle more easily: Fear Free Happy Homes preparing for the vet visit.

The American Kennel Club also notes that treats are part of your dog’s total daily intake, and many owners have heard the rule that treats should stay around 10% or less of the daily diet. If your dog has a sensitive stomach or weight concerns, it is smart to plan rewards ahead of time and keep them modest: AKC on how many treats a dog can have.

Our practical tip is simple: bring more treats than you think you need, but make every piece very small.

Good session-day treat ideas

  • Soft training treats broken into tiny bits
  • Small pieces of a high-value food your dog already tolerates well
  • A lickable reward if your dog prefers that over crunching
  • Your dog’s regular kibble for easy moments, if they are food motivated

Photo day is not the best day to test a brand-new snack.

Bring one comfort item that smells like home

A small blanket, mat, or towel can do more than people expect. It gives your dog a familiar place to stand or settle, and it can help on benches, patios, or studio floors. Fear Free Happy Homes specifically recommends bringing familiar bedding or cloth items with comforting scent associations, which is one reason we like this so much for pet photo sessions.

If your dog knows how to relax on a mat, even better. The AKC has a helpful overview of teaching a dog to go to their place or mat, which can make waiting between photos feel clearer and calmer for many dogs: AKC go to your place training.

This does not need to be polished obedience. We only want your dog to feel like they have a safe little home base.

Make the car ride safe and boring

The drive to the session sets the tone. The American Veterinary Medical Association advises pet owners to safely restrain dogs in vehicles with a secure, fitted harness or other appropriate restraint, keep them out of the front seat if airbags are present, and never leave pets unattended in a car. Their travel guidance is worth a quick read before any outing: AVMA traveling with your dog or cat.

For many dogs, a calmer ride also means fewer surprises. Load up early, keep the temperature comfortable, and skip extra errands on the way if you can. We want your dog arriving ready to sniff, stretch, and start, not arriving overstimulated from a long chain of stops.

Before you pull out of the driveway

  • Offer a potty break
  • Secure your dog with a fitted restraint or carrier
  • Pack water and a bowl
  • Bring cleanup bags and a towel
  • Check that tags and leash are on and easy to reach

Plan for weather, even on mild days

Georgia weather can turn a simple outing into a tiring one faster than expected. Heat is the big issue most of the year, but damp ground, chilly wind, and sudden rain can matter too.

The AVMA warns never to leave a pet in a car, even in the shade or with cracked windows, and notes that warm-weather exercise can be riskier for some dogs, including overweight pets and short-nosed breeds. Their warm-weather pet safety page is a good reference for common-sense precautions: AVMA warm weather pet safety.

For outdoor portraits, we suggest bringing water, allowing shade breaks, and being flexible. If your dog starts to slow down, pant heavily, or seem less interested in rewards, that is useful information. Comfort comes first.

Pack for little problems, not big productions

You do not need a giant gear bag. A few smart basics are enough.

The AVMA recommends a simple travel-sized pet first aid kit and keeping key emergency phone numbers with you when traveling with pets. Their first aid tips for pet owners include a practical checklist that can help you build a small kit: AVMA first aid tips for pet owners.

A sensible photo-session bag

  • Tiny treats
  • Water and a collapsible bowl
  • Leash and backup leash if needed
  • Waste bags
  • Towel or mat
  • Brush for quick touch-ups
  • Any medication your veterinarian has already prescribed for travel or anxiety
  • A small first aid kit and emergency contact numbers

If your dog has medical or behavior concerns, session day is not the time to improvise. General prep tips are helpful, but they do not replace advice from your veterinarian or a qualified trainer.

Leave room for pauses

Some of the best portraits happen after a short break. A minute to sniff the grass, drink water, or stand next to you can reset a dog who is trying hard. We would much rather work in short, calm bursts than push for nonstop posing.

That is especially true for young dogs, senior dogs, and dogs who are still learning how to be comfortable in new places. A good session pace respects the dog in front of us.

The goal is not perfection

Your dog does not need to perform like a show dog to make beautiful images. A soft expression, a head tilt, muddy whiskers, a crooked sit, or the way they lean into your leg can matter more than a textbook pose.

A simple day-of plan helps because it protects your dog’s energy for the parts that count. Safe travel, tiny treats, familiar comforts, water, weather awareness, and time to settle can go a long way.
